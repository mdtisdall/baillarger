# Offsets, folds and self-contact: local vs global self-intersection

Scope: how CAD, computational geometry, cloth/shell simulation and seismic/structural geology handle an offset or a thin layer meeting itself, and what they offer for telling local (curvature-caused) self-intersection from global (distant-part) self-intersection.

Tags: [verified] = I read it in the cited source during this session (often abstract, search-result quote or HTML page, not always the full paper). [unverified] = from my background knowledge or a secondary summary that I could not check against the primary text. [inference] = my reasoning, not a sourced claim.

## 1. Offset curves/surfaces in CAD: local vs global self-intersection

### Takeaway
CAD uses a fixed two-way split. Local self-intersection happens when the offset distance exceeds the smallest radius of curvature on the concave side; the offset map becomes singular (cusps / cuspidal edges). Global self-intersection happens when two distinct, distant parts of the progenitor are within twice the offset distance; the distance between two distinct points has a local minimum. Detection follows the split: curvature tests or offset-map singularity for local, distance-pair computations (distance maps, bisectors, rolling sphere with two contact points) for global. Both kinds are trimmed with the same machinery.

### Cited Findings
- [verified] Definitions as usually quoted, attributed to Maekawa 1999: the offset "may self-intersect locally when the absolute value of the offset distance exceeds the minimum radius of curvatures in the concave regions", and it "can self-intersect globally when the distance between two distinct points on the curve reaches a local minimum." Quoted in a search result for — [Kumar et al., arXiv 1303.3578](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1303.3578), citing Maekawa, "An overview of offset curves and surfaces", *Computer-Aided Design* 31(3):165–173, 1999.
- [verified] Seong, Elber & Kim, "Trimming local and global self-intersections in offset curves/surfaces using distance maps", *CAD* 2005. They build a rational distance map between the progenitor and its offset. For curves, the self-intersection regions are the zero set of a bivariate polynomial; for surfaces, of a system of three polynomials. The zero set is projected to parameter space for trimming, and numerical marching finishes the job. The same tool handles local and global self-intersections. — [author PDF](https://gershon.cs.technion.ac.il/papers/offset_srf_trim.pdf) (abstract-level; full text not readable here); companion curve paper by Elber: [offset_crv_trim.pdf](https://gershon.cs.technion.ac.il/papers/offset_crv_trim.pdf)
- [verified] Patrikalakis, Maekawa & Cho, *Shape Interrogation*, §11.3.2: offset surfaces have "two types of singularities ..., namely irregular points and self-intersections". The parameter-space curves where the offset is singular map to cuspidal edges, and those curves are "contour lines of constant principal curvatures" of the progenitor. An offset of a sphere at its radius collapses to the centre. At an umbilic, an offset at the matching distance forms a cusp, so all umbilics must be found to find all cusps. — [MIT hyperbook node225](https://web.mit.edu/hyperbook/Patrikalakis-Maekawa-Cho/node225.html)
- [verified] Same book, §11.3.3: the self-intersection curve of an offset is the locus of the centre of a sphere whose radius is the offset distance, "rolling on the progenitor surface with two contact points". The two contact points are the "foot point curves of the self-intersection curves". For a hyperbolic cylinder, a self-intersection is attributed to "the global distance function property (constriction)" of the hyperbola, i.e. a global, not curvature, cause. — [MIT hyperbook node226](https://web.mit.edu/hyperbook/Patrikalakis-Maekawa-Cho/node226.html)
- [verified] Chiang, Hoffmann & Lynch 1992, "How to compute offsets without self-intersection" (SPIE), is an early approach that avoids self-intersection rather than trimming it after the fact. Only the title was checked. — [Purdue PDF](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Chiang-International_society_for_optics_and_photonics-1992-How_to_Compute_Offsets_Without_Self_Intersection.pdf)
- [unverified] Standard differential geometry (Rodrigues' formula): along each principal direction of the progenitor, the offset map x ↦ x + d·n scales tangent vectors by a factor (1 − d·κ_i), with sign conventions that depend on the normal orientation. The offset map is singular where d equals a principal radius (a focal point), and its Jacobian determinant changes sign past it, which is an orientation flip. This matches the hyperbook's "constant principal curvature" contours above, but I did not read the equation in a primary source this session.
- [unverified] For planar curves, a local self-intersection loop is bounded by two cusps of the offset (a "swallowtail"). A global loop has no cusps; it is formed by two regular arcs crossing. This is commonly stated in the offset-trimming literature (Elber & Cohen; Maekawa), but I did not confirm it in a primary text here.
- [unverified] Pham (1992, *CAD*) and Farouki & Neff (1990, *CAGD*, "Analytic properties of plane offset curves"; "Algebraic properties ...") are the classic references on offset singularities and their algebraic degree. Not retrieved this session.

### Inferences
- [inference] There are two diagnostics, and they need different data. Local: the Jacobian of the offset map (or of the front propagation) changes sign or vanishes at the meeting, equivalently d ≥ 1/κ_max on the concave side near the foot point. Global: two foot points that are far apart along the progenitor, with a distance-to-surface function that has a local minimum between them.
- [inference] The split is not fully intrinsic; it depends on scale. A tight fold whose hinge radius r is smaller than the thickness T gives a local (focal) self-intersection near the hinge. Further from the hinge, the same event continues into a pair of nearly parallel, distant-foot-point limbs, which is global-like. In parameter space the self-intersection region is connected to the singular (cusp) locus. A "kissing banks" contact has no singular locus nearby. A topological criterion is therefore whether the self-intersection region in parameter space meets the curve where the offset Jacobian vanishes. A threshold on geodesic foot-point separation alone is scale-dependent.
- [inference] The distance-map formulation (Seong et al.) suits a front-tracking project because it works with pairs of foot points (s,t) on the progenitor. A meeting voxel can be tagged with its two foot points on the white surface, and both their geodesic distance and the curvature along the path between them can be computed.

### Gaps
- I could not read the exact wording or formulas in Maekawa 1999 or Seong et al. 2005 (PDF text not extractable here). I did not verify whether either paper states that the local/global split is scale-dependent, or gives a rule for the borderline case.
- No source found that gives a quantitative "same fold vs two banks" criterion in CAD. CAD trims both kinds the same way and does not need to tell them apart.

## 2. Offset of a closed surface = boundary of a dilation; medial axis, reach, local feature size

### Takeaway
The untrimmed-free offset at distance d is the d-level set of the distance function, i.e. the boundary of the dilation by a ball of radius d. Its self-intersections are cut away exactly where the front crosses the medial axis (the set of points with more than one closest point). Reach (Federer) and local feature size (distance to the medial axis) are the scale bounds below which an offset is guaranteed to be free of local and global self-intersection. Local feature size is a pointwise mix of curvature and distant-part proximity; it does not separate the two causes by itself.

### Cited Findings
- [verified] The medial axis of a closed set S is the set of points that do not have a unique closest point on S. The local feature size lfs(p) is the distance from p to the medial axis. The reach is the infimum of lfs over S. Reach was introduced by Federer; local feature size was rediscovered and named by Amenta & Bern. lfs is 1-Lipschitz. A "local reach" measured only in directions orthogonal to M at y satisfies rch(y,M) ≥ lfs(y). — [arXiv 1803.07642](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.07642); [arXiv 2206.10485](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.10485) (from search-result summaries of these papers)
- [verified] Mérigot et al. work with r-tubular neighbourhoods (offsets) of compact sets and show that Federer's curvature measures of a set with positive reach can be estimated stably from a Hausdorff approximation. — [Chazal, Cohen-Steiner, Lieutier, Mérigot, Thibert, arXiv 1001.2961](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1001.2961)
- [verified] Federer proved that reach is stable under C^{1,1} diffeomorphisms of the ambient space. — [arXiv 1803.07642 / 2206.10485 summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.10485)
- [unverified] Federer (1959, "Curvature measures"): for d < reach, every point within distance d has a unique nearest point, so the normal map (x, t·n) ↦ x + t·n is injective on the tube (normal injectivity radius). For a C² closed surface, reach ≤ min principal radius of curvature, and reach is also bounded by half the smallest "bottleneck" distance between distinct parts. This is the standard decomposition of reach into a local (curvature) and a global (bottleneck) term, as used e.g. by Aamari et al. Not read in a primary source this session.
- [unverified] Medial-axis structure (Blum; Giblin & Kimia): generic interior points of a medial sheet have exactly two foot points. Medial-sheet boundaries ("rim" / edge points) are where the two foot points merge into one, i.e. a focal point (centre of a principal curvature maximum, a ridge of the surface).

### Inferences
- [inference] This gives a local, data-free classification of a front meeting point m. Compute its foot points on the white surface (the "rolling sphere with two contact points" from §1).
  - True global contact ("kissing banks"): two well-separated foot points p ≠ q, the meeting is in a medial-sheet interior, and d(m) < focal radius at both p and q (the offset map is still orientation-preserving at both).
  - Local focusing: the foot points coalesce, the meeting is at or near the medial edge (a focal point), and the offset Jacobian vanishes or changes sign.
  - Continuous transition: the medial sheet of a tight fold ends at the hinge's centre of curvature, so following a meeting point along the sheet towards its edge is a principled way to find where "fold" becomes "contact".
- [inference] The trimmed offset (the boundary of the dilation) automatically "trims via the medial axis". In a voxel growth, the meeting surface of the front is the part of the white surface's exterior medial axis that is closer than T. This is the voxel analogue of CAD trimming.
- [inference] For apposed banks with a thin fluid gap g, the medial sheet sits in the gap, at distance (T + g/2) from both white surfaces. A "true contact" signature is therefore a meeting where the two fronts arrive nearly antiparallel (n(p)·n(q) ≈ −1). A focal meeting has n(p) ≈ n(q) at coalescing foot points.

### Gaps
- No source found that uses medial-axis edge distance explicitly as a "fold vs contact" classifier. This is my inference.
- I did not retrieve a primary statement of the curvature-plus-bottleneck decomposition of reach (Aamari, Kim, Chazal, Michel, Rinaldo, Wasserman, "Estimating the reach of a manifold", EJS 2019 would be the place to check).

## 3. Thin shells, cloth, folded sheets: self-contact and offset meshing

### Takeaway
Cloth simulation detects self-contact by proximity (a thickness or repulsion distance) between non-adjacent primitives, plus continuous collision detection. Topological adjacency on the mesh is the only "local" exclusion: primitives that share vertices are not tested. Folding a sheet onto itself is treated the same as two sheets meeting. When intersections already exist, history-free methods (Global Intersection Analysis) use the topology of the intersection curves to decide which side each vertex is on. Offset-meshing and thickening tools resolve self-intersections by building the offset as an isosurface of a distance field, i.e. the dilation, then remeshing.

### Cited Findings
- [verified] Bridson, Fedkiw & Anderson, SIGGRAPH 2002 (*ACM TOG* 21(3):594–603), combine a fail-safe geometric collision method with a fast, non-stiff repulsion force that models cloth thickness and static/kinetic friction. Simultaneous collision clusters are resolved as rigid impact zones. — [Stanford project page](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/cloth-sig02/); [PDF](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~rbridson/docs/cloth2002.pdf)
- [verified] Harmon, Vouga, Tamstorf & Grinspun, "Robust treatment of simultaneous collisions" (SIGGRAPH 2008). The rigid-impact-zone fail-safe prevents collisions but removes all relative tangential velocity and unphysically dissipates energy. They propose a fail-safe that stops impact without stopping sliding, plus approximate Coulomb friction. — [Columbia PDF](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/cg/pdfs/RTSC.pdf); [SIGGRAPH archive](https://history.siggraph.org/?p=112828)
- [verified] Baraff, Witkin & Kass, "Untangling Cloth" (SIGGRAPH 2003). It is history-free. Global Intersection Analysis finds the intersection curves between meshes. For each curve, a flood fill colours both sides and the smaller region is kept. That region colouring decides which vertex pairs should attract and which should repel, so already-intersecting cloth is pulled back through itself. — [Pixar library](https://graphics.pixar.com/library/UntanglingCloth); [course summary](https://research.cs.wisc.edu/graphics/Courses/777-s2006/TextWeb/Summaries/Week11/krill-untangle.txt); patents [US 6970171](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6970171)
- [verified] Selle et al. 2009 extend the collision/friction approach to high-resolution cloth with history-based repulsion. — [PDF](https://naml.us/paper/selle2009_cloth.pdf)
- [verified] Zint, Maruani, Rouxel-Labbé & Alliez, "Feature-preserving offset mesh generation from topology-adapted octrees" (*CGF* 42(5), SGP 2023). Inputs are a triangle mesh or soup, an offset radius and a maximum normal deviation. They dual-contour the offset on an adaptive octree, then remesh. — [EG digital library PDF](https://diglib7.eg.org/bitstream/handle/10.1111/cgf14906/v42i5_10_14906.pdf)
- [verified] Offsetting each element directly can create self-intersections at sharp features, and these are costly to resolve. — [Cardiff PDF](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/188761/4/3832781.pdf). Later work ("Robust and Feature-Preserving Offset Meshing", [arXiv 2412.15564](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.15564)) reports that the Zint et al. code could not do inward offsets. "Topological Offsets" (NYU 2025) extracts the offset as an isosurface and remeshes to remove self-intersections. — [PDF](https://cims.nyu.edu/gcl/papers/2025-Topological-Offsets.pdf)
- [verified] Inward + outward offsetting to make a shell solid of finite thickness is used for additive manufacturing. — [arXiv 2310.08997](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.08997)

### Inferences
- [inference] Cloth has the same ambiguity as the project, but resolves it with time history: CCD checks contacts at each step, so a fold never passes through itself. The side of each contact is the sign of the signed distance at the start of the step, a "local definition of sides" inherited from the last valid state. For a growing front, the analogous rule: each front element carries its own outward normal (the offset map's orientation), and a meeting has two sides defined by the two arriving normals, with no global labelling needed.
- [inference] The cloth exclusion rule (skip primitives that are topologically adjacent within k rings) is the discrete version of "foot points too close along the surface to count as a contact". The k-ring size is a hand-set scale. Cloth practice confirms that the local/global split is set by a length scale (thickness vs mesh-adjacency radius), not intrinsically.
- [inference] GIA's "smaller region" rule is a global, topology-based tie-breaker. It is the opposite of what the project wants (local sides), but useful as a fallback check: a meeting surface that encloses a small patch of the front is probably a local fold/focus artefact.

### Gaps
- I did not read the full Bridson et al. or Harmon et al. papers for how they handle thickness when a sheet is folded tighter than its thickness (the analogue of local focusing). Neither abstract addresses it.
- No source found on cloth/shell methods that classify self-contacts as "fold hinge" vs "distant contact"; they treat both the same.

## 4. Geology / seismic: same horizon folded back vs different horizons in contact

### Takeaway
Seismic interpretation assumes horizons are single-valued in depth (RGT volumes, phase unwrapping, flattening). Overturned or recumbent folds break this; industry handles them with multi-valued ("multi-Z") horizon tracking and rule-based truncation. Structural implicit modelling handles tight and overturned folds by building a fold frame (axial surface, fold axis, limb rotation angle) and interpolating stratigraphy in that frame. The "same horizon folded back" identity is carried by the fold frame and by continuity through the hinge, not by local reflector appearance.

### Cited Findings
- [verified] Stark: an RGT volume stores a geologic-time estimate at every seismic sample. Phase unwrapping assumes a surface of constant instantaneous phase is a horizon of constant geologic time (Stark 2003 SEG abstract; "Relative geologic time (age) volumes", *The Leading Edge* 23:928–932, 2004). A related patent notes that geologic time increases with travel time or depth, "with a few exceptions". — [SEG 2004 abstract page](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2004/annual/abstracts/Stark.htm); [US 6850845](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6850845); citation via [arXiv 2605.01273](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.01273)
- [verified] In overturned beds, reverse faults and salt bodies, different parts of one structure cross the same trace more than once. Conventional pickers handle this poorly; interpreters patch overlapping segments. The multi-Z auto-tracking patents seed each surface, build a bounding "data hull" for it, track inside it, check each surface against geological boundary rules, and truncate violating surfaces. — [US 11294085](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11294085); [US 11340369](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11340369)
- [verified] GeoChron (Mallet) maps a geological model to a chronostratigraphic space (u, v paleogeographic; t geologic time) in which fold and fault deformation are removed; it formalises Wheeler's time-stratigraphy idea. — [patent "Systems and methods to build sedimentary attributes"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9477010); [EGU25-16567](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU25/EGU25-16567.html)
- [verified] Grose, Ailleres, Laurent & Jessell, "LoopStructural 1.0: time-aware geological modelling" (*GMD* 14:3915–3937, 2021). Folds are modelled in a fold frame whose major element is the axial foliation, with the fold axis as structural direction. Geometry is set by a fold-axis rotation angle and a fold-limb rotation angle that is 0 at the hinge and positive/negative on the two limbs. "Folds are challenging to model using classical implicit interpolation algorithms, because by definition a folded surface has a symmetry only defined by their axial surface." Refolded structures are modelled youngest first. Faults use a separate fault frame (fault surface + slip direction) and displacement kinematics. — [GMD article](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/14/3915/2021/gmd-14-3915-2021.html)
- [verified] Laurent et al. 2016, "Implicit modeling of folds and overprinting deformation" (*EPSL*), builds the fold coordinate system from axial foliations, intersection lineations and vergence. — [HAL record](https://hal.univ-lorraine.fr/hal-01390858) (full text blocked; abstract-level)
- [verified] Learned RGT methods note that RGT is a constrained field where "local errors readily propagate globally". — [Dou et al., arXiv 2605.01273](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.01273)

### Inferences
- [inference] Geology's analogue of the project's split:
  - Fold hinge = continuous horizon with a rotating normal through high curvature, so the limb rotation angle passes through 0. This is "local" self-meeting.
  - Fault = displacement discontinuity, which the project's "no faults in cortex" rule excludes.
  - Two limbs of an isoclinal fold in contact = "global" apposition with the same stratigraphic age.
  - Two different horizons in contact = an unconformity, or a fault with a younging-direction reversal.
- [inference] Geology tells "same horizon folded back" from "different horizons" mainly by younging direction (stratigraphic polarity). On a folded-back limb the age gradient flips relative to depth, but it stays continuous along the layer through the hinge. The cortical analogue of polarity is the orientation of the depth coordinate (white→pial). Two apposed outer (pial) surfaces have opposite depth gradients meeting head-on (n_p·n_q ≈ −1, both fronts at depth = 1), the "same horizon in contact" case. A tight-fold focus has the depth gradient collapsing (Jacobian → 0) without a second distinct front.
- [inference] The fold-frame idea (a hinge-centred coordinate whose sign marks the limb) is a ready-made local "sides" label: the sign of the limb angle, or of the offset-map orientation, labels the two sides of a meeting without a global segmentation.

- I found no paper on how seismic interpreters decide, from data alone, whether two touching reflectors are one horizon folded back or two horizons. The multi-Z patents use user-seeded surfaces and rule checks. I could not read Laurent 2016 or Mallet's GeoChron papers in full for how overturned limbs (non-monotone t in depth) are parameterised.

## 5. Defining the "sides" of a contact locally

### Takeaway
No surveyed field states a general "local sides" definition as such. The ingredients are standard: (a) the orientation of the offset map (Jacobian sign), (b) the two foot points and their normals of the rolling sphere / medial point, and (c) in cloth, the signed distance and normal at the last collision-free state. Together these give a local, label-free rule.

### Cited Findings
- [verified] Self-intersection points of an offset correspond to a sphere of the offset radius touching the progenitor at two contact points (foot points). — [MIT hyperbook node226](https://web.mit.edu/hyperbook/Patrikalakis-Maekawa-Cho/node226.html)
- [verified] Medial-axis points are exactly those with more than one closest point on the set. — [arXiv 1803.07642](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.07642)
- [verified] Offset singularities (cuspidal edges) lie on contours of constant principal curvature of the progenitor. — [MIT hyperbook node225](https://web.mit.edu/hyperbook/Patrikalakis-Maekawa-Cho/node225.html)
- [verified] Cloth repulsion and collision response act along contact normals with a modelled thickness; GIA uses intersection-curve topology for history-free sides. — [Bridson et al. 2002](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/cloth-sig02/); [Untangling Cloth](https://graphics.pixar.com/library/UntanglingCloth)

### Inferences
- [inference] Proposed local criterion for a meeting voxel m in a grown front:
  1. Get the foot points p, q on the white surface (from the propagation's origin labels or a nearest-point transform).
  2. Sides = {front arriving from p, front arriving from q}. The side normals are the front normals at arrival (or n(p), n(q)).
  3. True contact if p and q are distinct, the arriving normals are roughly antiparallel, and the offset map is still orientation-preserving (non-focal) along both normal paths up to m, i.e. d(m) < principal focal radius at p and at q.
  4. Local focusing if the arrival normals are close to parallel, p and q converge as one moves along the meeting set, or the Jacobian of the depth/offset map vanishes or flips before m.
  5. Mixed (tight fold whose limbs then appose): the meeting set is connected to a focal edge. Classify per point by the rule above rather than per meeting component.
- [inference] A thin hidden fluid gap does not change the rule. It moves the meeting surface to the middle of the gap and keeps the normals antiparallel. A focus has no such gap and shows a vanishing Jacobian.

### Gaps
- No source found that validates such a rule on folded thin layers. It is a synthesis of the definitions above and needs testing on the project's data.
