# Cross-domain precedents (flattening / RGT / variable-speed eikonal) and fused-sulcus handling in cortical depth frames

Verification key: "PMID-verified" = bibliographic match returned by PubMed citation lookup in this session. "Page-verified" = title/authors/venue/DOI read on the publisher, PMC, or repository page in this session. "Unverified" = from memory or a search snippet only; check before citing.

## Q1. Has anyone applied seismic flattening / horizon tracking / RGT / Wheeler-domain ideas to cortex or other layered tissue?

### Takeaway
I found no published work that applies seismic flattening, RGT, or Wheeler-domain methods to cerebral cortex, and none that cites the seismic literature for any biological tissue. That absence is a finding. The nearest analogue is in retinal OCT. There, "flattening" to a reference boundary (usually Bruch's membrane) is a standard preprocessing step, and Carass et al. 2014 go further with a learned column-wise "flat space" in which segmentation runs and is then mapped back. Both are explicit, per-A-scan (1-D column) remappings between two bounding surfaces. Neither is a fitted global scalar field whose isosurfaces follow the image contrast, which is what seismic RGT is.

### Cited Findings
**Seismic side (definitions, for the report writer)**
- Seismic flattening maps a volume from depth/two-way time to the "Wheeler domain" in geologic time. Horizons are then isosurfaces of one RGT volume: "An arbitrary number of horizons then can be extracted as isosurfaces of RGT values." Slope-based flattening "often fail[s] to flatten the reflections in a global sense and cannot accurately align the reflections across faults." — [Wu et al. 2022, "Toward accurate seismic flattening: Methods and applications" (PDF, USTC CIG)](http://cig.ustc.edu.cn/_upload/tpl/05/cd/1485/template1485/papers/wu2022flat.pdf) (page-verified as a PDF on the authors' group site; the journal venue was not confirmed in this session)
- Flattening framed as finding a depth function that makes each layer constant, "sometimes referred to as geological time." — [Seismic volumetric flattening and segmentation (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/253383293_Seismic_volumetric_flattening_and_segmentation) (authors and venue not confirmed in this session)
- Related RGT precedents: [Relative geologic time (age) volumes — relating every seismic sample to a geologically reasonable horizon (Stark)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249868018_Relative_geologic_time_age_volumes-Relating_every_seismic_sample_to_a_geologically_reasonable_horizon); [3D graph-cut phase unwrapping RGT with horizon and unconformity constraints](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258647372_Generating_a_relative_geologic_time_volume_by_3D_graph-cut_phase_unwrapping_method_with_horizon_and_unconformity_constraints); [Deep Relative Geologic Time (Bi et al. 2021)](http://cig.ustc.edu.cn/_upload/tpl/05/cd/1485/template1485/papers/bi2021deepRgt.pdf) (titles from search; full bibliographic details not checked in this session)
- A broad web search combining seismic flattening/RGT with medical or layered-tissue terms returned only geophysics and applied mathematics papers, with no medical application. — [search performed 2026-10-26; representative hits above]

**Retinal OCT: flattening as preprocessing (well established)**
- Lang et al. 2013 flatten each B-scan to Bruch's membrane (BrM). They estimate ILM and BrM from Sobel vertical gradients after Gaussian smoothing (σ = 3 px), replace outliers more than 15 px from the median-filtered surface, smooth the BrM with Gaussian σ(x,z) = (20, 2) px, and then **translate each A-scan** so that the BrM is flat. This is done once from initial estimates, and the main algorithm refines the boundaries later. Their stated rationale is that spatial features get a consistent meaning, the search region is constrained, and sensitivity to curvature and orientation drops. They call flattening "common" and cite Garvin 2009, Chiu 2010, and Mayer 2010 for it. — [Lang A, Carass A, Hauser M, Sotirchos ES, Calabresi PA, Ying HS, Prince JL. "Retinal layer segmentation of macular OCT images using boundary classification." Biomed Opt Express 2013;4(7):1133–1152. doi:10.1364/BOE.4.001133 (PMC3704094)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3704094) (page-verified)
- **"Flat space" (Carass et al. 2014)** is the closest analogue to a Wheeler domain in biomedical imaging. ILM and BrM are estimated first. From 37 manual segmentations they learn the mean *proportional* position (BrM = 0, ILM = 1) of each of 7 internal boundaries, per fovea-aligned A-scan. Each A-scan is then resampled by linear interpolation to 89 samples between BrM and ILM, plus 10 px of margin on each side (109 px per column). Random-forest boundary probabilities are resampled into flat space, the multi-object geometric deformable model (MGDM) runs there, and results map back to native space with subvoxel boundaries. Their stated benefits: layers are nearly flat, so the curvature term acts as a natural regularizer; boundaries can come closer than one voxel apart; and computation drops. The paper does not attribute the idea to prior flattening work. — [Carass A, Lang A, Hauser M, Calabresi PA, Ying HS, Prince JL. "Multiple-object geometric deformable model for segmentation of macular OCT." Biomed Opt Express 2014;5(4):1062–1074. doi:10.1364/BOE.5.001062 (PMC3986003); correction in BOE 6(4):1351 (2015)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3986003) (page-verified)
- Graph-search / optimal-surface lineage that flattening is used with:
  - Garvin MK, Abràmoff MD, Kardon R, Russell SR, Wu X, Sonka M. "Intraretinal layer segmentation of macular optical coherence tomography images using optimal 3-D graph search." IEEE TMI 2008;27(10):1495–1505, PMID 18815101. Five layers, minimum-cost closed set with smoothness and surface-interaction constraints, border error 6.1 ± 2.9 µm. — [PubMed lookup](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18815101/); [Iowa repository](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Intraretinal-Layer-Segmentation-of-Macular-Optical/9983806267002771) (PMID-verified; DOI not checked)
  - Garvin MK et al. "Automated 3-D intraretinal layer segmentation of macular spectral-domain OCT images." IEEE TMI 2009;28(9):1436–1447, PMID 19278927. Seven surfaces, error 5.69 ± 2.41 µm against interobserver variability of 5.71 ± 1.98 µm; varying feasibility constraints. Lang 2013 cites it for flattening. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19278927/); [Iowa repository](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Automated-3-D-Intraretinal-Layer-Segmentation-of/9983806266802771) (PMID-verified)
  - Chiu SJ et al. "Automatic segmentation of seven retinal layers in SDOCT images congruent with expert manual segmentation." Opt Express 2010;18(18):19413–19428, PMID 20940837. Graph theory plus dynamic programming. Lang 2013 cites it for flattening. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20940837/) (PMID-verified; its flattening details were not read in this session)
  - Li K, Wu X, Chen DZ, Sonka M. "Optimal surface segmentation in volumetric images — a graph-theoretic approach." IEEE TPAMI 2006;28(1):119–134, PMID 16402624. This is the foundation of LOGISMOS-style multi-surface graph search. Its column structure requires a terrain-like (height-field) surface, so it implicitly needs a flattened or unfolded domain. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16402624/) (PMID-verified; the claim about the column requirement is my summary of the method, not quoted)
- A patent on retinal fluid segmentation describes flattening to the ILM before segmentation. — [USPTO 10251550](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10251550)

**Ice-sheet radiostratigraphy (non-biological layered medium, close to RGT)**
- Radar internal reflecting horizons are treated as isochrones. Ages from ice cores are propagated along continuous horizons. Ng & King (2011) model how "the depth and slope of isochrones" propagate across a radargram and retrieve layer ages from steady isochrone patterns. This is a slope-field age model similar to slope-based seismic RGT. — [AntArchitecture review (NSF PAR)](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10608403-review-article-antarcticas-internal-architecture-towards-radiostratigraphically-informed-agedepth-model-antarctic-ice-sheets); [Ng & King 2011 (White Rose eprints)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/43293/) (venue details not checked)

### Inferences
- OCT practice shows that a two-surface column remapping (shift to one boundary, or proportional resampling between two) gives most of the benefit of a Wheeler domain when layers are nearly parallel and columns are well defined. Cortex breaks both assumptions at high curvature and in fused sulci. This supports going further, to a fitted scalar T rather than a column remap.
- Carass's proportional "flat space" is the OCT counterpart of an equidistant cortical depth. The equivolume (Bok) correction used in cortex (Waehnert 2014; Leprince 2015, see Q2) has no OCT counterpart because retinal curvature is small. Neither field fits T to the *internal* layer contrast. That step, fitting isosurfaces to the data rather than to the boundaries, appears to be the novel part of the user's proposal.
- Seismic RGT methods that handle faults and unconformities (graph-cut phase unwrapping with constraints, deep RGT) are the closest technical analogue to a cortical seam, where T is discontinuous or has two branches. No biomedical paper treats the sulcal seam this way.

### Gaps
- I did not search tree rings, skin OCT, or histology (e.g., BigBrain laminar segmentation) for flattening or RGT language. BigBrain work (Wagstyl et al.) fits laminar surfaces from intensity profiles and should be checked separately; it was not covered here.
- I did not read how Garvin 2009, Chiu 2010, and Mayer 2010 each flatten (Lang 2013 only cites them as using it).
- Venues and DOIs for the seismic papers were not verified.

## Q2. Variable-speed eikonal / travel-time / PDE depth and thickness in cortex: does any method weight speed by intensity so that depth follows layers?

### Takeaway
The standard PDE frameworks (Laplace streamlines, Yezzi–Prince Eulerian thickness, Rocha hybrid, Acosta Lagrangian–Eulerian with partial-volume maps, the Waehnert/Bazin equivolume model, the Leprince Laplacian-equivolumic model) all set depth from **the two bounding surfaces and the geometry between them**, not from internal laminar contrast. The only intensity-dependent "speed" I found is in boundary finding: ACE's CSF-weighted distance and DELFMAP's GM-probability-weighted permittivity. Both serve to locate the sulcal seam, not to bend depth isosurfaces toward layers. I found no method that weights the eikonal speed by internal laminar intensity so that depth isosurfaces follow, for example, the stria of Gennari or the Baillarger bands.

### Cited Findings
- Yezzi & Prince 2003. Thickness is the length of correspondence trajectories along a smooth vector field (the Laplace gradient), computed with two upwind first-order PDEs whose sum gives thickness, with no landmarks or parameterization. — [Georgia Tech repository record, "An Eulerian PDE approach for computing tissue thickness," Oct 2003](https://repository.gatech.edu/entities/publication/b0159a94-1b8a-457e-ba62-d728d13cd34a). Venue: IEEE TMI 22(10), 2003, likely PMID 14552586 (PubMed lookup returned two candidate PMIDs, 14552586 and 12715991, and I did not check which is which). DOI 10.1109/TMI.2003.817775 is **unverified** (from memory). Earlier version: [CVPR 2001, "A PDE approach for measuring tissue thickness"](https://mlanthology.org/cvpr/2001/yezzi2001cvpr-pde/).
- Rocha KR, Yezzi AJ, Prince JL. "A hybrid Eulerian–Lagrangian approach for thickness, correspondence, and gridding of annular tissues." IEEE Trans Image Process 2007;16(3):636–648, PMID 17357725. It combines Eulerian speed with Lagrangian accuracy. — [Georgia Tech repository](https://repository.gatech.edu/entities/publication/dea1a29e-91e5-4a57-9a6e-f54d60628e32) (PMID-verified; pages from memory)

Med Image Anal 2009;13(5):730–743, doi:10.1016/j.media.2009.07.003, PMID 19648050, PMC3068613. It notes that two banks in contact within a voxel "may appear connected if the CSF is not detected," and uses partial-volume maps to recover sulcal delineation (Fig. 15). — [PMC3068613](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3068613); [Fig. 15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3068613/figure/F15) (PMID-verified). ### Inferences
- DELFMAP is the most direct precedent for a "variable-speed" field in cortex. It weights a Laplace-type operator by tissue probability so that the field's ridge (the seam) lands where the data show the fundus. It weights by tissue *class* (GM vs. CSF), not by laminar intensity inside GM.
- A field T whose isosurfaces follow laminar contrast would be new in the cortical literature. The closest conceptual neighbours are OCT flat space (column remap) and seismic RGT (fitted global scalar).

### Gaps
- I did not find or verify Haidar or Bazin papers on anisotropic or intensity-driven depth. Bazin's "topology-preserving tissue classification" and "Nighres" (Huntenburg et al. 2018) were not checked.
- BigBrain laminar work (Wagstyl et al. 2018, 2020) may use intensity-profile-driven surface placement. It was not checked here and is the most likely place to find an intensity-guided laminar coordinate.
- I did not confirm the Yezzi–Prince 2003 TMI DOI or its page numbers.

## Q3. How do cortical depth frames handle kissing, fused, or buried sulci? Is the eikonal shock or medial axis ever the seam? Ex vivo methods?

### Takeaway
Tools split into two camps. **Topology and self-intersection approaches** (FreeSurfer, CLASP) keep the two banks' pial surfaces from crossing. **Explicit seam approaches** (CRUISE/ACE, DELFMAP) compute a medial "separating barrier" inside the fused GM and impose it as a constraint on the pial surface. ACE takes the skeleton of a CSF-weighted distance function. DELFMAP (Osechinskiy & Kruggel) takes the **shock** of the distance/correspondence field, and states explicitly that streamlines from opposite banks collide there. DELFMAP was validated on **ex vivo hemispheres at 0.25–0.35 mm isotropic**, so it is the closest published precedent for the user's setting and for "the eikonal shock is the seam." A *bridged* sulcus (a handle) is a topology problem. A *buried* sulcus (unresolved fundus, no change in topology) is purely geometric, which is why a barrier is needed.

### Cited Findings
- **CRUISE / ACE.** Han X, Pham DL, Tosun D, Rettmann ME, Xu C, Prince JL. "CRUISE: cortical reconstruction using implicit surface evolution." NeuroImage 2004;23(3):997–1012, PMID 15528100. It reconstructs inner, central, and outer surfaces with a topology-preserving geometric deformable model. ACE places a thin separating barrier in sulci, derived from the skeleton of a weighted distance function with CSF-membership-based speed. — [PubMed 15528100](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15528100/); [USUHS record](https://scholar.usuhs.edu/en/publications/cruise-cortical-reconstruction-using-implicit-surface-evolution/); [Nighres cruise_cortex_extraction](https://nighres.readthedocs.io/en/latest/cortex/cruise_cortex_extraction.html) (PMID-verified; DOI 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2004.06.043 from memory, unverified). Tosun et al. (2004, NeuroImage) on cortical surface segmentation and mapping is also relevant — [PDF](http://iacl.ece.jhu.edu/~prince/pubs/2004-NI-Tosun-IPAM.pdf) (not read).
- **Bridged vs. buried terminology.** A bridged sulcus creates a handle that a topology-preserving model can fix. A buried sulcus (unresolved fundus) does not change topology, so it needs a barrier. — [Osechinskiy & Kruggel 2012 (PMC3296314)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3296314/)
- **DELFMAP (key precedent).** Osechinskiy S, Kruggel F. "Cortical surface reconstruction from high-resolution MR brain images." Int J Biomed Imaging 2012;2012:870196, doi:10.1155/2012/870196, PMC3296314. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3296314/) (page-verified)
  - Field: an electric potential in an inhomogeneous dielectric. **Dirichlet BCs: potential 1 in the WM core and 0 on the image boundary.** GM permittivity is tied to GM probability. It is solved as the steady state of a diffusion-like equation.
  - Seam: "streamlines from opposite cortical banks collide inside sulci," causing **shocks in the distance field and discontinuities in correspondence**. Detection works two ways: (a) a shock test where |∇distance| < 0.8; (b) a novel correspondence test, where correspondences of neighbouring points (6-neighbourhood) differ by more than 4 voxels. The skeleton of the sulcal medial surface then acts as a barrier for the evolving pial surface.
  - Compared with ACE, DELFMAP skeletons are more regular. ACE skeletons have small extraneous branches and gaps, and ACE needs a minimum-distance cutoff near WM to suppress spurious detections. DELFMAP was more robust to noise in simulations.
  - Data: **three explanted left hemispheres at 0.25–0.35 mm isotropic** (ex vivo), and also OASIS at standard resolution compared with FreeSurfer.
  - Conference precursor: Osechinskiy S, Kruggel F. "Identifying intrasulcal medial surfaces for anatomically consistent reconstruction of the cerebral cortex." Proc SPIE 7962, Medical Imaging 2011, 79620N, doi:10.1117/12.877863. It claims to need no tunable parameters. — [SPIE](https://spiedigitallibrary.org/conference-proceedings-of-spie/7962/79620N/Identifying-intrasulcal-medial-surfaces-for-anatomically-consistent-reconstruction-of-the/10.1117/12.877863.full) (DOI from URL)
- **FreeSurfer**, as Osechinskiy & Kruggel describe it: topological defect correction of the initial white surface plus a self-intersection prevention routine that separates banks in tight sulci. They note that self-intersection checks are expensive and scale poorly, and that FreeSurfer conforms data to 1 mm. — [PMC3296314](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3296314/) (secondary description; FreeSurfer primary papers not read)
- **CLASP.** Kim JS, Singh V, Lee JK, Lerch J, Ad-Dab'bagh Y, MacDonald D, Lee JM, Kim SI, Evans AC. "Automated 3-D extraction and evaluation of the inner and outer cortical surfaces using a Laplacian map and partial volume effect classification." NeuroImage 2005;27(1):210–221, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2005.03.036, PMID 15896981. A Laplacian map between WM and a skeletonized CSF guides the expansion of the GM surface from the WM surface. On synthesized thick cortex the error was 0.5 ± 0.4 mm, against 4.6 ± 1.4 mm for ASP. — [SNU repository](https://s-space.snu.ac.kr/handle/10371/11567); [PubMed 15896981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15896981/) (PMID-verified). **Unverified:** whether CLASP's outer Laplace boundary is the skeletonized CSF. I recall that it is, but the sources retrieved here did not confirm it.
- **Acosta 2009 / MICCAI 2008.** Partial-volume maps recover unseen CSF in contact banks, and the MICCAI 2008 work corrects sulci by homotopic thinning. — [PMC3068613](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3068613); [MICCAI 2008 PDF](https://iacl.ece.jhu.edu/proceedings/miccai2008/52410253.pdf)
- **Deep learning plus Laplace for deep sulci.** An arXiv paper (2303.00795) states that partial volume fuses tight sulci into "bridged" or "unresolved" sulci, which biases thickness. It incorporates Laplace's equation into a DL framework to segment deep sulci. — [arXiv 2303.00795](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.00795) (authors and venue not read; I believe it is from the UPenn PICSL group on ex vivo MTL, unverified)
- **Nighres, LayNii, highres-cortex.** Their docs describe depth computed from given inner and outer boundaries (level sets or a rim segmentation). I found no dedicated kissing-sulcus mechanism in their layering modules; seam handling is delegated to the segmentation (in Nighres, the CRUISE/ACE step). — [Nighres volumetric_layering](https://nighres.readthedocs.io/en/latest/laminar/volumetric_layering.html); [LayNii](https://github.com/layerfMRI/LAYNII); [highres-cortex](https://github.com/neurospin/highres-cortex)

### Inferences
- The user's current pipeline (fast-marching D from white, shock ridge, separate seam placement) reproduces the DELFMAP diagnosis exactly: the shock of the distance/correspondence field *is* the medial seam. DELFMAP's refinement is to weight the field by tissue probability, so the shock moves from the geometric midline to the dark CSF remnant. That is analogous to the user's min-cut on darkness. A weighted-speed fast march (speed lowered in dark voxels) would move the collision ridge toward the dark seam in the same way, merging the two steps.
- Laplace with interior boundary conditions: CLASP (if it does use skeletonized CSF as the outer Dirichlet boundary) and CRUISE/ACE plus Nighres (barrier imposed, then depth between the two level sets) both use the seam *as the outer boundary* once it is found. That amounts to a Dirichlet condition (depth = 1, pial) on the seam. I found no paper that imposes a Neumann condition on the seam or treats it as a free boundary to be solved jointly with depth.
- For a fitted T in fused cortex, the seismic "fault" analogy holds: T is single-valued in each bank and meets at the seam with equal value (both sides pial). That suggests a Dirichlet seam on T, as in CRUISE-style pipelines.

### Gaps
- I did not read the FreeSurfer primary papers (Dale, Fischl, Sereno 1999; Fischl & Dale 2000) or the CLASP full text for details on sulcal CSF skeletonization.
- I did not find ex vivo methods at 100–200 µm that target kissing gyri other than DELFMAP (0.25–0.35 mm) and the arXiv DL+Laplace paper. Ex vivo pipelines from UPenn and MGH (e.g., Wisse, Ravikumar, Yushkevich; Costantini et al. 2023 for ex vivo surfaces) were not checked.
- No source was found that treats the seam as a Neumann boundary, or that formulates depth and seam jointly (for example, a two-phase level set with a shared interface).
